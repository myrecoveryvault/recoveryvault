FIELD REPORT // PERSONAL TELEMETRY

The Long Way to a Bumper Sticker

Why an analytical mind had to take the watch apart before the slogans could work.

"Early on, I was handed the phrase 'Keep it simple.' The problem was, my brain is not simple—and every time I tried to force simplicity, I just found more elaborate ways to fail."

I. The Friction with "Keep It Simple"

Early on in the rooms, I was handed an entire wall of slogans:

  • • One day at a time.
  • • Turn it over.
  • • Keep it simple.
  • • Let go and let God.

If those work for you right out of the gate, take them and run. Truly.

For me, though, trying to live by those phrases felt like trying to stop a runaway freight train with a bumper sticker. The issue was never that the slogans were wrong—they are entirely accurate, and they keep thousands of people sober every single day. The issue was that my brain simply doesn't run simple. Every time I tried to force simplicity before I actually understood what was happening under the hood, I just ended up engineering more elaborate, intellectual ways to fail.

I found I couldn't just adopt a phrase on blind compliance; I had to understand how the machinery worked before I could trust it.

II. Understanding → Conviction → Application

What I eventually realized about my own wiring is that I don’t operate well on top-down commands. My head requires a very specific sequence before real change happens:

1. Understanding

I have to take the watch apart on the workbench. I need to see every gear, every broken tooth, and the hidden motives driving my reactions.

2. Conviction

That uncomfortable, inescapable moment where I look at my own patterns on paper and realize my old way of managing life is completely bankrupt. I can no longer talk myself into believing my own rationalizations.

3. Application

Only after conviction takes hold can I actually take the action in the real world.

I built this system because I kept trying—and failing—to skip straight to Stage 3. I couldn’t honestly "turn something over" until I thoroughly mapped out my compulsive instinct to control it. I couldn’t live "one day at a time" until I caught my brain twenty-four days into the future, obsessively scripting conversations that hadn't happened yet.

The strange, humbling conclusion of doing all this heavy, structured work across hundreds of notes and ledgers is that it brought me right back to the bumper stickers. But for me, there was a massive difference between reading a slogan on a clubhouse wall and arriving at that exact same truth through bloody-knuckled work. Today, when I say One day at a time, it isn’t a platitude I'm repeating to sound good in a meeting—it is an operational survival rule backed by months of visible, documented evidence in my own life.

III. Rumination vs. Meditation (Rails on an Overactive Mind)

Step 11 talks about prayer and meditation, and for a long time, the meditation piece was a brick wall for me. People suggested I "quiet my mind," "empty my thoughts," or "just sit in the stillness." But for an alcoholic brain prone to racing, sitting in a quiet room with an empty head felt like being locked in a dark alley with my worst enemy.

The breakthrough came when someone pointed out what biblical meditation historically looked like. In the original language of the Psalms, the word for meditate (Hagah) paints the physical picture of an animal chewing the cud—what biologists call rumination.

That hit home immediately. I already knew how to ruminate. My brain had been doing it unconsciously for decades: picking up an insult, a financial panic, a fear of the future, or an old resentment, dragging it back up into my head, and chewing on it all day long until I had thoroughly poisoned my own day.

What I found for myself is that meditation is just rumination with rails.

Unbounded Rumination

Re-chewing resentment, fear, and self-justification in a closed, isolated loop that leads right back to paralysis, anger, or a drink.

Bounded Meditation

Deliberately taking an objective principle, a Step prompt, or an honest inventory item and chewing on that—turning it over repeatedly within safe boundaries until the truth digests and actually shifts my behavior.

I didn't need to force my brain to stop thinking; I needed to give it an honest track to run on. If your head works anything like mine, maybe you've felt that exact same tension.

IV. An Objective Workbench

To do that kind of work, an ordinary journal didn't cut it for me. A blank diary too easily became a place where I just wrote down feelings about feelings, quietly justifying my resentments and convincing myself I was "processing." And commercial wellness apps never felt right—I wasn't interested in putting my most vulnerable inventory into a proprietary cloud database that locked my thoughts behind a subscription or spit out generic, sterilized pop-ups.

I needed a private, plain-text workbench on my own computer. Something local, permanent, and structured enough to catch my blind spots, trace patterns across months, and hold up an honest mirror. Most importantly, I needed a tool that knew when to tell me to shut the screen, get out of my head, and go take action in the physical world.

It is definitely the long way around to get to a simple truth. But for a mind wired like mine, taking the long way was the only thing that actually held.
